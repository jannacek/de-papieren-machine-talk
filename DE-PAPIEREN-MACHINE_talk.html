I want to start with a sentence. It’s from the afterword Marvin Minsky wrote in 1984 for Vernor Vinge’s novella True Names. Minsky helped found the field of artificial intelligence, so he’s allowed to say this.

[Let the first line finish. Pause.] “In real life, you often have to deal with things you don’t completely understand.” [click] “You drive a car, not knowing how its engine works.”

[Pause.] Hold on to that sentence. For the next eighteen minutes I’m going to agree with it — and then argue with what it leaves out.

He asks

His answer, in short

“It is a great idea, that use of symbols. It lets our minds transform the strange into the commonplace.”

He asks the obvious next question: how do we cope? [click] His answer is lovely. When something is too strange or too complicated to deal with directly, we use a symbol — an icon, a name, a handle — so that it looks like something we already know. [click, click] “It is a great idea, that use of symbols. It lets our minds transform the strange into the commonplace.”

[click] I think he’s right. That’s what a steering wheel is. That’s what a desktop icon is. [click] And I’d like you to notice: the strangest technology we’ve built is wearing the most familiar thing we have. A conversation. A chat window.

Two definitions of “AI literacy”
EU AI Act · Art. 3(56)

“…skills, knowledge and understanding that allow providers, deployers and affected persons … to make an informed deployment of AI systems, as well as to gain awareness about the opportunities and risks of AI and possible harm it can cause.”

Long & Magerko · CHI 2020

“…a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace.”

Look at the verbs: a very well-informed passenger.
Where is decide? Refuse?
Make differently?

Which brings me to literacy. “AI literacy” is suddenly everywhere, so here are two definitions that run a lot of the world. The European AI Act, Article 3(56): skills, knowledge and understanding that let people make an “informed deployment” of AI systems and “gain awareness” of the opportunities and risks. And Duri Long and Brian Magerko, at Georgia Tech, 2020 — the one everyone cites: competencies that let you “critically evaluate” AI, “communicate and collaborate” with it, and “use” it as a tool.

[click] These are good definitions. I’m not here to sneer. But look at the verbs. Deploy. Be aware. Evaluate. Collaborate. Use. [click] That’s a very well-informed passenger. [click] Where’s decide? Where’s refuse? Where’s make it differently?

[Say if challenged: I mean the headline definitions, the ones policy picks up. The research field goes further than this.]

Why now? Three quick reasons. [click] One: it’s law. Since February 2025 the AI Act has an article on AI literacy, and this July it was recalibrated, not removed. When literacy becomes an obligation, it gets standardised — into a module, a quiz, a certificate. We should care which literacy gets standardised.

[click] Two: the interface. The better the wheel feels, the less anyone asks what’s under it. That’s Minsky’s trick working perfectly.

[click] Three: it’s where we look things up. Search used to hand you a list and let you choose. More and more it hands you a sentence, and doesn’t show its working. A library is a place that has always shown its working.

So where are the decisions, if they aren’t in the chat box? Before you type anything. [click ×3] What’s in the archive — and what somebody left out. What counts as a unit: a word, a piece of a word. What counts as a good next word: what the system is trained to want. [click] And running underneath: who labelled what, under whose categories.

[click] Nobody sees these. That’s not a conspiracy; it’s an interface doing exactly what Minsky said interfaces do. But each of them is a human decision, made at a particular time by particular people. So each one can be named. And each one can be different.

I’m saying this in a library, which is lucky, because libraries have been making these decisions in public for centuries. The KB was founded in 1798. By its own account, until 1974 there was no real strategy behind what it collected. [click ×3] And look at how it’s described: a keyword system called KB-Code, from 1921 to 1992. Brinkman, from 1974 to now. A third, GOO and NBC, from the 1980s to 2012. [click ×2] All three are still in the catalogue.

[click] Three ways of describing the same shelves, three different centuries’ ideas of what a subject is, sitting side by side. None of it is a scandal; each was the standard of its time. That’s the point. A decision made in 1921 doesn’t announce itself in 2026. It just keeps quietly shaping what can be found.

It’s a language model made of paper. A very small, very old kind: a next-word predictor. Give it a word, it guesses the next one — by counting. You take a page of a Dutch newspaper from Delpher, the KB’s digitised archive; the part we use runs from 1618 to 1879, old enough to be public.

[click] You read it through a slot, so you can’t read ahead. [click] You copy it out, one word per cell. And you decide what counts as a word — a hyphenated one, a broken one. That choice is the tokenisation. [click] You cut: every pair of neighbouring words becomes a scrap. [click] You file the scraps on a big board by their first word. That board is the model.

To make it speak: pick a word, reach into its pile, draw blind. The second word is your next word. Go to its pile. Draw again.

Three operations build it, and each one is a decision wearing a costume. [click] Corpus: whose pages? Somebody chose them — in our case me and the KB team. [click] Tokenise: what counts as a word? There’s no right answer. [click] Predict: draw blind. It says only what it was fed.

[click] And there’s a fourth operation running alongside. Classify: you sort the same material with the library’s own keyword systems — 1921, 1974, the eighties. What you build is an index. It’s real, it’s useful — [click] and in the workshop it never touches the machine. I keep it separate on purpose, because people constantly blur “sorting the world” and “predicting the next word”.

Now the part I care about most. At the end, there are two kinds of silence on the table. [click] The first: the scrap that was never in the machine. A page nobody chose, a word nobody could read. That’s excluded. Somebody decided — and you can name who, and when.

[click] The second: the scrap that’s in the machine, in the pile, and just never got drawn. It was available. It was only improbable. That’s available but unsaid.

[click] Here’s why I make people separate them. When a system is criticised for leaving something out, the critic is usually pointing at the first silence. And the defence is nearly always the second: “it’s in there, it just didn’t come up — it’s probabilistic.” [click] Sometimes that’s true. And sometimes the first silence is wearing the second one’s clothes. You can only ask which one it is if you’ve held both kinds of paper in your hands.

The people who build the machine hang them in the KB’s window — one pink line, one teal — and they stay up until the end of January.

Let me be honest about what a pile of paper is not. It is not how ChatGPT works. [click] This machine sees one word of context. A modern language model sees long stretches of text, learns far richer structure than word pairs, and has billions of parameters instead of a few hundred scraps. If I told you this workshop explains GPT, you should leave.

[click] What survives the scale-up — what a toy can honestly show — is this: someone chose the material. Someone chose the units. Someone chose what counts as good. Someone chose what stays out. A paper machine can’t tell you what GPT does. It can tell you where to point your questions.

[click] And it’s a very old idea. Andrey Markov counted, by hand, how vowels and consonants follow each other in Pushkin’s Eugene Onegin in 1913; Claude Shannon wrote out word-pair English in 1948. We’re one more person with scissors.

So that’s the workshop. Now the stance: why I make things this way, as an artist and as a researcher. My research question has three words in it, and they’re the three things I think an artistic situation can do that a lecture can’t.

[click] Perceptible: make the decision something you can see and hold — a scrap of paper in your hand. [click] Questionable: make it something a stranger at your table can ask about. Who cut this? So here’s what I’m arguing. [click] One: Minsky is right. You don’t need to understand the engine. Nobody understands everything. A literacy that demands a mechanic’s licence from everyone is a fantasy.

[click] Two: you do need standing. The standing to ask who decided, to say no, to imagine it otherwise.

[click] Three: standing isn’t taught. It’s practised — at a table where the decisions are visible and you’re one of the people making them. Even at a tiny scale. Even with scissors.

[click] And four: that’s the job of an artist and a researcher. Build the table. And be honest about its silences.
