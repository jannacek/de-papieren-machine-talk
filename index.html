What AI literacy is supposed to be
EU AI Act · Art. 3(56)

“‘AI literacy’ means skills, knowledge and understanding that allow providers, deployers and affected persons, taking into account their respective rights and obligations in the context of this Regulation, to make an informed deployment of AI systems, as well as to gain awareness about the opportunities and risks of AI and possible harm it can cause.”

Long & Magerko · CHI 2020

“…a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace.”

Same paper, design consideration 2: “embodied simulations of algorithms and/or hands-on physical experimentation”
Read only the verbs: a skilled user. Minsky’s driver.
Where do people practise it — deciding, refusing, changing?
That is the literacy my research is after.

So: AI literacy. It’s suddenly everywhere, so here are two definitions that run a lot of the world. The AI Act: skills, knowledge and understanding that let people make an “informed deployment” of AI and “gain awareness” of its risks. Long and Magerko, Georgia Tech, 2020 — the one everyone cites: “critically evaluate”, “communicate and collaborate”, “use”.

[click] Read only the verbs and you get a skilled user — Minsky’s driver. [click] But read on, and they ask for more. The Act speaks of affected persons and their rights. Long and Magerko recommend “embodied simulations of algorithms” and hands-on experiment — which is pretty much tonight.

[click] So the ambition is there. What’s rare is the practice: where do people actually get to decide, to refuse, to change a decision — and see what happens? [the last line follows by itself] That’s the literacy my research is after.

So where are the decisions, if they aren’t in the chat box? Before you type anything. [click] What’s in the archive — and what somebody left out. What counts as a unit: a word, a piece of a word. What counts as a good next word: what the system is trained to want.

[click] And this is what you see: a box that says “Ask anything.”

[click] All of this happens before you type. Running underneath: who labelled what, under whose categories. Nobody sees these. That’s not a conspiracy; it’s an interface doing exactly what Minsky said interfaces do. But each of them is a human decision, made at a particular time by particular people. So each one can be named. And each one can be different.

Why is this urgent? Three reasons. [click] One: it’s law. Since February 2025 the AI Act has an AI-literacy duty. When literacy becomes an obligation, it gets standardised: into a module, a quiz, a certificate. And the standard version is the driver’s version.

[click] Two: it’s an interface. The better the wheel feels, the less anyone asks what’s under it. That’s Minsky’s symbols, working perfectly.

[click] Three: it’s where we look things up. Search used to hand you a list and let you choose. More and more it hands you a sentence, and doesn’t show its working. A module won’t fix that.

Where does this workshop come from? Partly from a name. One of today’s big chatbots is called Claude. [click] It’s reportedly named after Claude Shannon — the engineer who, in 1948, wrote the paper that founded information theory. And in that paper, there’s this.

[Read slowly, flat:] “The head and in frontal attack on an English writer that the character of this point is therefore another method for the letters…” A sentence nobody wrote.

[click] He made it by hand: open a book, find a word, write down the word that comes after it; another page, find that word, write down what follows. A language model with a book and a pencil — almost eighty years before the chatbot that carries his name.

I come to this from another direction. During my master’s I did this with old neural networks — trained on the theses in my own institute’s archive, and I made a book of what they wrote. [Optional: “Of Gostak and Doshes” — the title comes from “the gostak distims the doshes”.] Now I’m doing it with a bigram model: the simplest one there is, made of paper, inspired by Shannon’s paper.

[click] That’s the workshop. I added a library, a few dozen strangers, and coloured cards.

House rules: lowercase · every punctuation mark is a word · numbers in letters · spelling as printed · don’t correct · every piece starts and ends with a full stop

Every one of those moves has a name in AI. [click] I chose the pages: sourcing. You pick one sentence: selection.

[click] You choose its colour: labelling — in the industry, people deciding piece by piece what a text is, often fast and paid per item. You may meet racist or colonial language; these papers are more than 140 years old. Leave it out, and that’s filtering. And the house rules — lowercase, every comma is a word, spelling as printed — that’s tokenisation. Someone always decides those. Here, it was me.

[click] Filing the card is training. Drawing blind is sampling. The model card is documentation; the next group is a new version. Every one of these is a human decision — and every one could be different.

Let me be honest about what this is not. It’s not how ChatGPT works. [click] This machine remembers only the previous word, and it can’t read. ChatGPT makes the same kind of prediction — but it looks back thousands of words instead of one, and it stores what it learned as numbers, not cards. If I told you this workshop explains ChatGPT, you should leave.

[click] What survives the scale-up is this: someone chose the material. Someone labelled it. Someone filtered it. Someone decided what a word is. A paper machine can’t tell you what ChatGPT does. It can tell you where to point your questions.
